01

Reverse bias,
drawn out.

A PN junction is two kinds of silicon side by side. Where they meet, something forms that you can only see by drawing it: a thin region with no free carriers and a strong electric field. This site follows what a reverse voltage does to it.

p-type silicon carries mobile holes; n-type carries mobile electrons. Put them in contact and both diffuse across the junction and recombine. They leave behind dopant atoms locked in the crystal, now uncovered ions: ⊖ on the p-side, ⊕ on the n-side. That carrier-free zone is the depletion region. Its charge sets up a built-in voltage that stops further diffusion:

$$V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}=0.714\ \text{V}$$
Assumptions

Silicon at 300 K, ni = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, NA = ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, uniform doping on each side, every dopant ionised, one dimension.

02

Charge makes the field, the field makes the voltage

Everything about the junction follows from one chain of two integrals.

  1. Charge ρ(x). −qNA over xp, +qND over xn. Equal areas: $N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n$.
  2. Field E(x). Gauss's law, integrate ρ/εs: a triangle, zero at the edges, largest at the junction, pointing n → p. $E_{max}=qN_Ax_p/\varepsilon_s$
  3. Potential V(x). Integrate E: two parabolas. The triangle's area is the whole voltage: $V_{bi}+V_R=\tfrac12E_{max}W$
Assumptions

Depletion approximation: no free carriers inside −xp … xn, exactly neutral outside, sharp edges. A full numerical solution softens the edges over a few Debye lengths (≈ 40 nm here) but barely changes W.

03

Reverse bias: build it, then measure it

Connect the junction to the supply the "wrong" way: n to +, p to −. The supply pulls holes and electrons away from the junction, more ions are uncovered, and the depletion region widens. More charge, stronger field:

$$W=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\Big(\frac1{N_A}+\frac1{N_D}\Big)(V_{bi}+V_R)}$$ $$E_{max}=\frac{2(V_{bi}+V_R)}{W}$$
  1. Wire it. Use the Wire tool: drag a cable from the supply's + to the junction's N, and from P to −.
  2. Turn it up. Raise the supply. Watch the depletion region widen and the field arrows grow, and the graphs below.
  3. Is a current flowing? Where would you put an ammeter to find out?
  4. Measure. The ammeter goes in series, inside the loop: unplug one cable and run it through IN and OUT. Then read it.
  5. Breakdown. Keep turning past about 103 V. The peak field reaches Ecrit ≈ 4·10⁵ V/cm and an avalanche starts: the current jumps from picoamps to the supply's 1 mA limit, and the voltage stops rising. Sparks show it in the junction.
What the ammeter adds up, and what we simplify

Ideal (Shockley) diffusion current $I_s=qAn_i^2\big(\tfrac{D_p}{L_pN_D}+\tfrac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\big)\approx1.5$ fA. Constant in reverse bias.

Generation current $I_{gen}=qn_iWA/2\tau$: heat creates electron–hole pairs inside the depletion region and the field sweeps them out. It grows with W, like √(Vbi+VR). The ideal diode equation leaves it out, yet in silicon it is ~1000× larger.

Breakdown: near VBR both are multiplied by $M=1/(1-(V_R/V_{BR})^4)$ (Miller). $V_{BR}=\varepsilon_sE_{crit}^2/2qN_{eff}-V_{bi}\approx103$ V. We take one fixed Ecrit; really it rises slowly with doping, and very heavily doped junctions break down by tunnelling (Zener) instead. Breakdown is not damage by itself if the current is limited, as our supply does.

Diode: 100 µm × 100 µm, Dn = 35, Dp = 12 cm²/s, lifetimes 1 µs. All voltage drops across the depletion region; no series resistance.

04

Beyond the step: the linearly graded junction

Junctions made by diffusion are not a sharp step. Near the junction the doping changes linearly, $N_D-N_A=a\,x$. Same chain, different shapes: ρ is a ramp, E a parabola, V a cubic.

The exponent is the fingerprint: on log–log axes W against (Vbi+VR) is a straight line of slope ½ or ⅓. A capacitance measurement (C = εA/W) reads it off a real diode.

Assumptions

Gradient a = 10²⁰ cm⁻⁴ (10¹⁶ cm⁻³ per µm); the depletion region is symmetric, ±W/2. $V_{bi}=\tfrac{2kT}{q}\ln\tfrac{aW_0}{2n_i}$, solved together with W₀: 0.667 V.
